# What a good brand book contains

Read at the direction stop, before you plan the pages. Based on about 150 sources checked in October 2026: published guidelines (NASA 1976, CMS, GOV.UK, NHS, Monzo, Klarna, Revolut, GoCardless, ING, Hilti, Mailchimp, Slack, Shopify, Atlassian), surveys of how guidelines are used, positioning and tone-of-voice writing, Polish procurement specifications and agency packages. Public guidelines disappear, so the useful parts are written down here instead of linked.

## 1. Who reads it

The users of an identity system aren't designers. They're the people who produce materials after the designer has left: the owner writing a post, a marketing coordinator, a sales manager, a printer, an agency hired later, working in a hurry and with no design background. In interviews with sixteen designers (Tarazona 2020), all of them included the logo, colours and type, and the sections they left out most were incorrect uses, print colours and master files: exactly what printers and non-designers need. Two tests belong in every project:

- **Before handover:** someone who didn't work on the brand makes a real piece (a post, a leaflet, an offer) using only the book. Every hesitation is a gap.
- **After six months:** see what was used and where the brand drifted. That is the brief for the next version.

Detail follows the reader: the owner and marketing get rules and examples, the designer and printer get values and files. The reader filter (owner, designer, printer, marketing) exists for this: send each the link to their view.

## 2. Names: what the client is asking for

| Name | Scope |
|---|---|
| Księga znaku (logo manual) | the logo only: versions, colours, clear space, minimum size, misuse |
| Visual identity system (SIW, KIW) | the logo plus colour, type, imagery and applications: stationery, slides, signage, packaging |
| Brand book | all of that plus strategy and voice: whom the brand serves instead of what, how it sounds, what it may promise |

Polish agencies use "brandbook" for anything from a three-page logo sheet to a full KIW, so the scope goes into the direction stop as a list of pages, not a name. Polish public procurement splits a "basic" manual (construction, CMYK/RGB/Pantone, clear space, mono and negative versions, minimum size, misuse, use next to partners' logos, stationery and the e-mail footer) from an "extended" one with applications (billboard, roll-up, vehicles, website). It's a good checklist for a small brand too.

## 3. The pages, one question each

Order by how often a page is needed, not by theory: the quick reference first, the brand next, then the rules. Strategy and voice open the book (NASA starts with a letter from the administrator; CMS puts tone right after the brand architecture).

| Page | The reader's question |
|---|---|
| Quick reference | Which logo, which colours, which font (and what in Word or Canva), the five rules, whom to ask, where the current files are? |
| Strategy | What does the brand do for whom, in one sentence? What do we do when the book doesn't say? |
| Positioning | What would the customer do instead of us, what goes wrong there, what do we do? What proves it, as of when? |
| Voice | What stays the same and what changes with the channel? Which words do we avoid? How do we write the name? |
| In practice | How do we write in the moments that matter (an enquiry, a quote, a refusal, a complaint)? The description to paste in one sentence and in fifty words; the lines that must appear |
| Applications | What does a post, a card, a mail, an offer look like? (Shown as mockups, not described.) |
| Imagery | Light, frame, people, props, styling, colour; may we use AI, and how is it marked? |
| Logo | Which version where? How much space? How small, on screen and in print? What not to do? How does it stand next to partners' logos? |
| Colour | Which values for which medium, in what proportions, which text on which background? |
| Type | Which face for what, which sizes, what to use in Word or Canva? |
| Labels | What goes on the front and in what order? Which content is mandatory and who approves it? |
| Print | Which profile, PDF standard, bleed, finishes? What to send the printer? |
| Files | Where is the right file, and which ones can go to print as they are? (On the quick reference and the print slide, not a page of paths.) |

Optional pages earn their place by use: icons and illustration when they're used every month, motion when there is video, signage and vehicles when there are vans, co-branding when partners appear. Open decisions are a handover list next to the book, not a page in it. Headlines say the topic and the rule in plain words ("Colours: yellow only on buttons"); a clever line the owner has to decode is worse than a topic name. A brand story and a technical manual have different readers; big brands add a short guide for partners (Revolut's is 14 slides, GoCardless's 15). For a small brand, one book in two clear parts is enough. An agency case study is a third genre: it tells the project to future clients and has no rules, so don't build the book's pages from one.

## 4. Text that works

**Rules, not adjectives.** "Use 16 px body text, never in capitals" is guidance; "be professional" is not. Each rule comes with its reason (so people can decide cases the book doesn't cover), a correct example, the typical mistake and the file. One well-chosen "not like this" teaches more than ten "like this".

**Three tests for every sentence of strategy and voice.** Put a competitor's name in it: if it still works, it says nothing (55% of the Fortune 100 list integrity as a core value). Ask what it rules out: a value or trait that forbids no sentence is decoration. Ask for the fact behind it: a claim without proof gets proof or goes.

**Positioning starts from the alternatives.** April Dunford's five parts, not a fill-in sentence ("For [target] who [need]…" assumes one answer per blank): what the customer would use instead (a bank, a dealer, doing it themselves, nothing), what the brand has that those don't, what that means for the customer with proof, who gets the most from it, and the category in which that is obvious. GoCardless writes it as a rule: "better than cards and manual bank transfers, without putting alternatives down".

**Voice as examples.** Three or four traits as "X, not Y" (Slack: "Confident (never cocky)"; Mailchimp: "weird but not inappropriate"), each with a sample line. A word list with replacements and reasons (GOV.UK: services are delivered, improvements aren't). Before-and-after rewrites from the brand's own copy (Monzo: "A decision has been made to close your account" becomes "We've decided to close your account"). Tone by situation, not only by channel: bad news leads with the impact, in the active voice, with who is responsible; never promise an outcome (Shopify). Voice sections of good guidelines run 150–600 words; length grows with the number of people who write.

**Ready-to-paste lines.** The description in one sentence and in about fifty words, built from proof, for a profile, a listing, an offer. The lines that must appear in a given place. How the name is written.

**What to cut.** Archetypes, brand onions and pyramids, the Golden Circle, a founder's manifesto, values every company shares, mood boards without rules, mockup galleries for bulk, pages explaining what a brand book is, construction grids when the client gets the vector file. Research, personas and competitor analysis belong in a strategy document, not in the book people work from.

## 5. Numbers you can check

| Topic | Rule |
|---|---|
| Length | no study fixes it; of 3,516 guides in one archive, 54% have up to 39 pages and 74% up to 59; practitioners give 10–20 pages for a small firm. Measure time to an answer instead: a HEX code in 30 seconds |
| Words | people read 20–28% of the words on a page (NN/g); Stripe's brand page is about 140 words, Shopify's 620, Hilti's 26-page guide 890, a good voice section 150–600. One rule per slide, at most about 90 words of prose, under about 800 for a small brand's whole book; tables of values don't count |
| Clear space | a part of the mark, called x, drawn on a diagram (Spotify: half the icon height; IBM: the height of the "I"); choose an element present in every version |
| Minimum size | in pairs: px for screen, mm for print, separately for the full logo and the symbol (Spotify: 70 px or 20 mm; the icon 21 px or 6 mm; ING: 12 px or 4 mm) |
| Foil and embossing | the minimum follows from the thinnest line the finish can hold; confirm with the converter |
| Colour | HEX and RGB for screens, CMYK with its profile, Pantone C and U when spot colours are printed, RAL or NCS for signs and vehicles (ING); rich black for large areas, 100% K for small text |
| Palette | 1–3 core colours, one accent, neutrals, written proportions; most brands use four or fewer main colours. Interface shades belong in `tokens.css`, not on the reader's first page |
| Contrast | WCAG 2.2: 4.5:1 for text, 3:1 for large text (18 pt, or 14 pt bold) and graphics, 7:1 for AAA; logotypes are exempt, text next to them isn't |
| Type | one or two families that differ in structure, plus a substitute for Word, Google Docs and Canva with the language's letters |
| Text size | body 10–12 pt in print, 15–25 px on screen; lines of 45–90 characters |
| Mandatory text on food labels | x-height at least 1.2 mm, 0.9 mm when the largest surface is under 80 cm² (Regulation (EU) 1169/2011, art. 13) |
| Bleed | 3 mm for offset by default; digital and label printers often ask 1.5–2 mm; confirm with the printer |
| Resolution | 300 ppi at final size; 600+ for labels; line art 1200 ppi |
| The PDF of the book | selectable text, version and date on the cover, small enough to email (aim under 20 MB) |

## 6. Patterns worth copying

- **NASA Graphics Standards Manual (1976):** the logo colour depends on the background in four classes; nine incorrect uses written as full sentences with a drawing each; a chapter of reproduction art (files, not pictures of files); a dated index of the pages in effect, so the binder is updated a page at a time.
- **CMS (Medicare, 2026):** a complete brand in 20 pages, tone of voice included, with a contact on the last page.
- **Klarna, partner guidelines:** organised by the situations a partner is in, with two principles: "The sender leads" and "Never blend identities".
- **GoCardless:** a vision in six words; positioning as a rule; "Bold statements are welcome. Empty ones aren't."
- **ING:** the colour table carries RAL and NCS next to Pantone and CMYK, for paint on signs and vehicles.
- **Hilti, distributor guidelines:** photos of real shops marked compliant and not compliant, and a date by which the old signage goes.
- **Spotify:** minimums for both media; clear space as half the icon; six short don'ts.
- **Slack:** every colour in four notations; a matrix of allowed and forbidden text and background pairs; a substitute font for office suites.
- **Mailchimp:** voice stays, tone changes with the reader's state; a TL;DR page; the name rule in one line ("one word, spelled with a big M and a little c").
- **Monzo:** three voice principles with an intensity per channel; no wit in operational messages about money; before-and-after rules.
- **Greenhouse:** photography as rules for light, composition, casting, props, wardrobe and grading.

## 7. Why brand books end up unused

Ranked by how many sources name the cause, with what this skill does about it:

1. **Nobody can find it.** In the only primary survey with the question (Demand Metric 2016, 234 responses), findability was the weakest-rated quality. The quick reference with `meta.home`, the same content as a web page and a PDF (organisations that enforce guidelines offer them in 2.4 formats on average, those that don't in 1.6).
2. **Too long, too much theory.** One question per page, rules as headlines, strategy cut to what changes a decision.
3. **No real situations.** Mockups from the tokens, situations from the brand's work, examples next to every rule. Canada.ca's analytics: the style guide and templates were the most used, and users asked "which one do I use" and "am I allowed to".
4. **Out of date.** Version and date on the cover, proof with dates, a changelog, an owner.
5. **No owner.** `meta.owner` on the cover; decisions with a person and a date. The managers' own example decides consistency (van den Bosch 2004).
6. **Written without the people who use it.** The handover test in section 1.

Statistics: "95% of companies have guidelines, 25% enforce them" comes from Demand Metric for Lucidpress (2016, 234 online responses), and the 95% includes 28% informal guidelines. "Consistent branding adds 23% (or 33%) to revenue" is the average of respondents' guesses to a hypothetical question, not a measurement. Neither belongs in a book or a proposal.

## 8. Polish specifics

- **Company data.** A sole trader gives the NIP in business statements, and an offer made at a distance carries the name, NIP and address (Prawo przedsiębiorców, art. 20). A limited company adds the court, KRS and share capital on letters, e-mails and the website (KSH, art. 206 § 1). Never KRS or capital on a sole trader's materials. Unknown values are placeholders in `01-verify.md`.
- **RODO in the e-mail footer** isn't mandatory when the information duty is met elsewhere; a short note with a link is the usual form.
- **Rights.** A transfer of copyright lists the fields of use; font licences and the logo's author belong on the files page. A missing logo author or source file is an open decision.
- **Editorial rules.** Quotation marks „…” (inside them «…»), a spaced en dash as a dash and an unspaced one in ranges (15–30%), a space in thousands (1 200 zł), a non-breaking space after one-letter words and before units. Every face and its office substitute must have ą ć ę ł ń ó ś ź ż.
- **Regulated claims.** Health claims on food follow the EU register (`references/labels-eu.md`). Promises about credit, leasing, approval or speed may carry information duties for an intermediary: the book says what the brand promises and where its fixed lines go, and `01-verify.md` asks a lawyer, never states the law.
